What the settings mean

SettingWhat it does
BitrateHow much data per second your stream uses. More looks better in busy scenes, but it has to fit your upload and the platform's limit. Constant bitrate (CBR) keeps it steady, which platforms want.
ResolutionHow many pixels. More pixels need more bitrate to stay sharp, so at a fixed bitrate a slightly lower resolution often looks cleaner in fast games.
Frame rate (FPS)Frames per second. 60 looks smooth for fast games; 30 halves the work and suits slow games and chatting.
EncoderWhat squashes the video. Graphics card encoders (NVIDIA NVENC, AMD, Intel QuickSync) barely touch your game's performance. The CPU (x264) can look great but is heavy on one PC.
PresetQuality versus effort. Slower presets look better but work the encoder harder. If OBS says "encoding overloaded", step one notch faster.
Keyframe intervalHow often a full picture is sent. Platforms want every 2 seconds.
Base and output resolutionBase (canvas) is what you build your scenes on. Output is what's sent. OBS shrinks one to the other with the downscale filter.

Test it before you go live

  1. Put the settings in, then do a private test: Twitch's bandwidth test mode, an unlisted YouTube stream, or a short recording.
  2. While it runs, watch the bottom-right of OBS. The square should stay green and "dropped frames" near 0%.
  3. Check View → Stats (or the Stats dock):
    • Dropped frames (network): your internet can't keep up. Lower the bitrate.
    • Skipped frames (encoding lag): the encoder can't keep up. Use a faster preset, or turn off Multipass.
    • Missed frames (rendering lag): the graphics card is maxed out. Cap your game's frame rate, or lower the game's settings.
  4. Play your busiest game moment and watch it back. Blocky in fast action? Drop resolution a step before dropping frame rate.
